# Mechanisms of Skill Acquisition and the Law of Practice

> [!info] A. Newell; P. S. Rosenbloom · 1981 · Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition

## Summary
> [!note] AI-drafted from secondary descriptions of the chapter — a base to refine.
**TL;DR** — The classic cognitive-science chapter (in Anderson, ed., *Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition*, pp. 1–55) arguing that a single *power law of practice* describes human skill improvement, and proposing the *chunking* theory of learning to explain it.
**Problem** — What functional form governs how performance (e.g., reaction time) improves with practice, and what cognitive mechanism produces it?
**Method** — (1) Present empirical practice curves across tasks; (2) analyze the mathematics of power-law functions; (3) compare three classes of functions against the data, arguing the power law fits best; (4) review existing models of practice; and (5) introduce the chunking theory as the underlying mechanism.
**Key results** — Establishes the power law of practice as a near-universal empirical regularity and offers chunking (building larger perceptual/action units) as its mechanistic account.

## Takeaways
- Origin of the widely cited "power law of practice" and of chunking as a learning mechanism (later central to the Soar architecture).
- A behavioral/cognitive-science grounding for how learning curves scale with experience.
- Relevant when motivating why learned components improve with practice/data rather than for control theory per se.
