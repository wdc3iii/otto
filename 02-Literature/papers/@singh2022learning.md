# Learning Bipedal Walking On Planned Footsteps For Humanoid Robots

> [!info] Singh, Rohan P.; Benallegue, Mehdi; Morisawa, Mitsuharu; Cisneros, Rafael; Kanehiro, Fumio · 2022 · 2022 IEEE-RAS 21st International Conference on Humanoid Robots (Humanoids)

## Summary
> [!note] AI-drafted from the abstract — a base to refine.
**TL;DR** — An RL policy that follows a given footstep plan achieves omnidirectional humanoid walking, turning, standing, and stair-climbing simply by conditioning on the next two planned steps.
**Problem** — Real-world humanoid deployment needs robust, user-commandable walking in any direction on 2D and 3D terrains; end-to-end RL policies typically lack this controllability without reference motions or pre-trained weights.
**Method** — Train a policy to track a footstep plan generated by procedurally sampling step sequences, feeding the upcoming two steps as input. A terrain-complexity curriculum drives learning and removes the need for reference trajectories or warm-started weights.
**Key results** — Feeding just the next two steps suffices for omnidirectional walking, turning in place, standing, and stair climbing; demonstrated in MuJoCo on two new humanoid platforms, HRP-5P and JVRC-1 (code released).

## Takeaways
- Two-step lookahead is a sweet spot: enough plan context for omnidirectional gaits without full-horizon planning.
- Curriculum over terrain complexity replaces the usual crutches of reference motions / pre-trained weights.
- Decouples footstep planning from the learned tracker — a plan-then-track structure rather than end-to-end perception-to-torque.

## Abstract (from bib)
Deep reinforcement learning (RL) based controllers for legged robots have demonstrated impressive ro-bustness for walking in different environments for several robot platforms. To enable the application of RL policies for humanoid robots in real-world settings, it is crucial to build a system that can achieve robust walking in any direction, on 2D and 3D terrains, and be controllable by a user-command. In this paper, we tackle this problem by learning a policy to follow a given step sequence. The policy is trained with the help of a set of procedurally generated step sequences (also called footstep plans).
